Electromagnetic radiation §7.1

A wave with two linked rulers

Wavelength (λ, distance between crests) and frequency (ν, cycles per second, Hz) are tied by the speed of light:

c = λν = 3.00×10⁸ m/s

Long wavelength means low frequency. The visible band (about 400–700 nm) is a sliver of the full electromagnetic spectrum.

The nature of matter §7.1

Light comes in packets

Planck: energy is absorbed or released by atoms only in discrete amounts called quanta. The energy and frequency of radiation are linked by

E = hν,   h = 6.626×10⁻³⁴ J·s

Einstein: electromagnetic radiation is itself quantized — a stream of “particles” called photons. The energy of one photon is

Ephoton = hν = hc/λ

So light has a dual nature: it shows both wavelike and particle-like properties.

Bohr's theory of the hydrogen atom §7.3

Quantized energy levels

White light gives a continuous spectrum, but an excited gas emits only a line spectrum — proof that the electron's energy is quantized. The allowed levels have energy

Eₙ = −2.18×10⁻¹⁸ J × (1/n²)

A jump between levels absorbs or emits a photon of energy |ΔE| = 2.18×10⁻¹⁸ J × |1/nf² − 1/ni²|. The same idea in wavelength form is the Rydberg equation, 1/λ = Rₐ(1/n₁² − 1/n₂²). The ground state is n = 1; any higher level is an excited state, and n = ∞ (zero energy) is ionization. Bohr's model works only for one-electron atoms like hydrogen.

The quantum mechanical model of the atom §7.5

Orbitals, not orbits

Schrödinger's equation contains both wave and particle terms. Solving it gives a wavefunction ψ. Its square, ψ², gives the probability of finding the electron at a point (the electron density). A specific wavefunction is called an orbital — and an orbital is not an orbit: it describes a distribution of electron density, not a path.

Quantum numbers §7.6

Numbers that name every electron

Schrödinger's equation requires three quantum numbers to describe an orbital:

n (principal)

1, 2, 3 … — shell; size and energy. Larger n is farther from the nucleus.

ℓ (angular)

0 … n−1 — subshell shape: 0=s, 1=p, 2=d, 3=f.

mₗ (magnetic)

−ℓ … +ℓ — orientation; a subshell has 2ℓ+1 orbitals.

A fourth number, the spin mₛ = ±½, is added with the Pauli principle (§7.7). A shell n holds n² orbitals and 2n² electrons; a subshell holds 2ℓ+1 orbitals and 2(2ℓ+1) electrons.

Orbital shapes and energies §7.7

Shapes of the subshells

Each subshell has a characteristic shape: s is spherical (1 orbital), p is a dumbbell (3 orbitals, start at n=2), d is mostly cloverleaf (5 orbitals, start at n=3), f is more complex (7 orbitals). Higher n means larger orbitals with more nodes (a node is where ψ² = 0; an s orbital has n−1 of them).

Electron spin & the Pauli principle: an electron also has a spin quantum number mₛ = ±½. The Pauli exclusion principle says no two electrons in an atom can share all four quantum numbers — so each orbital holds at most two electrons, and they must have opposite spins.

Electron configurations & orbital diagrams §7.8–7.9

Three rules fill the orbitals

Pauli

Max 2 electrons per orbital, with opposite spins.

Aufbau

Fill lowest-energy orbitals first (follow the diagonal).

Hund

Singly fill degenerate orbitals before pairing.

Watch the anomalies: Cr = [Ar]3d⁵4s¹ and Cu = [Ar]3d¹⁰4s¹ — a half-filled or filled d subshell is extra stable.

For an ion, start from the neutral atom: anions add electrons by Aufbau, while cations lose them from the highest-n shell first. For a transition metal that means the 4s electrons leave before any 3d — so Fe ([Ar]3d⁶4s²) becomes Fe²⁺ = [Ar]3d⁶, not [Ar]3d⁴4s².

Configurations, blocks & valence electrons §7.8–7.9

The table is a filling map

The periodic table is organized by the subshell being filled: groups 1–2 are the s-block, 13–18 the p-block, 3–12 the d-block (transition metals), and the bottom rows the f-block. An element's position gives its configuration directly.

Core electrons are the inner [noble-gas] electrons; valence electrons are those outside the noble-gas core and drive bonding. Elements in the same group share the same valence configuration — for p-block elements the d electrons are core, but for transition metals the d electrons count as valence.
